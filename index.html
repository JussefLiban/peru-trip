Saturday night · Chifa Titi

Chifa Titi. Chifa is what happened when Cantonese families settled in Peru in the 1800s and started cooking with what they found here — it is now as Peruvian as ceviche. Ask anyone in Lima where to eat chifa and Titi is the first name you get. The Chan family opened it in 1958 and three generations later they are still behind the wok.

What to order

Pato al sillao — roast duck in soy sauce, the dish people cross Lima for. If you know Peking duck, this is its Lima cousin: same crisp skin, finished in dark soy instead of hung for the pancake.

Sillao is simply the Peruvian word for soy sauce, borrowed straight from the Cantonese si-yau and now spoken by everyone in this country without a second thought. The word came over with the cooks and stayed. That is the whole history of chifa in five letters.

Dinner · Restaurante Hakka

Hakka is the name of the Chinese community most Chinese-Peruvian families come from — a fitting place to put both families around one table.

Mr. Chang left Canton with nothing — real poverty — and built a family and a life here in Peru. It is the same story the menu tells: Chinese hands, Peruvian ground, and years of work that paid off. Steffano, our head of finance, is where that story stands today, and in this house is treated as a nephew rather than a colleague.

Having the two of them at the table on Sunday night is not one more item on the week’s list. It is part of the reason for the week.

The last night · Astrid y Gastón

Gastón Acurio and Astrid Gutsche opened it in 1994 and then spent thirty years arguing, in public and with food, that Peruvian cooking belonged among the best in the world. They won that argument — the restaurant has sat on the World’s 50 Best list for years, and half the Peruvian chefs working today came through its kitchen. Since 2014 it lives in a colonial hacienda house in San Isidro.
